# HDS Conceptual Framework v1.0

**Status:** Foundational working model — proposed, not empirically validated.  
**Purpose:** Give HDS a shared vocabulary for research design, assessment, intervention development, and product decisions.

## 1. Working definition

Human Development Science (HDS) is a proposed interdisciplinary research and practice framework for understanding, developing, and evaluating human capabilities that help people function, adapt, learn, act, and contribute across changing life situations.

HDS is not currently an established scientific discipline, and this document does not claim that its framework is novel or validated. Its concepts must be compared with existing work in psychology, behavioral science, education, motor learning, exercise science, public health, sociology, and related fields.

## 2. The central question

**How can people develop capabilities that allow them to act effectively and sustainably across meaningful real-life situations — especially when those situations involve difficulty, uncertainty, pressure, competing goals, or changing conditions?**

HDS should study both:
- What capabilities people have, and how those capabilities develop.
- Whether, when, and why people can deploy them in real contexts.

The primary initial research emphasis is mental and behavioral capability. Physical-motor and social-environmental capabilities remain part of the wider model.

## 3. Three interacting domains of life

These are contexts in which capabilities are expressed, not isolated human faculties.

### A. Self
- Self-regulation and impulse management
- Emotion regulation and stress response
- Goal setting, planning, and follow-through
- Attention, metacognition, and self-awareness
- Learning, adaptation, and habit formation
- Persistence, recovery after setbacks, and help-seeking

### B. Environment and the wider world
- Situational awareness and decision-making
- Practical problem-solving and independent functioning
- Navigation, risk recognition, and safety
- Adaptation to unfamiliar or changing conditions
- Resource use and action under uncertainty

### C. Other people
- Communication and perspective-taking
- Cooperation, trust, and relationship maintenance
- Leadership, followership, and shared responsibility
- Conflict management, negotiation, and boundaries
- Social support and coordinated action

A capability may operate in more than one domain. For example, emotion regulation can matter when alone, in a dangerous environment, or during conflict with another person.

## 4. Three dimensions to examine

These dimensions interact; they should not be treated as independent silos.

1. **Mental / psychological / behavioral:** processes such as attention, appraisal, motivation, emotion regulation, self-control, confidence, decision-making, and action regulation.
2. **Physical / motor:** health-related fitness, strength, endurance, mobility, coordination, skill, and the ability to perform physical tasks.
3. **Values / character / social responsibility:** chosen principles and patterns such as responsibility, honesty, care, courage, fairness, and contribution.

Values are not to be inferred from performance scores. A high score on a challenge does not establish that a person is ethical, responsible, or courageous.

## 5. The knowledge–capability–performance distinction

HDS should explicitly distinguish at least three levels:

- **Knowledge:** a person knows or can explain what a task requires.
- **Capability:** a person can perform the relevant action under specified conditions.
- **Real-world performance:** a person actually performs it over time in a particular context.

These levels can diverge. Knowing a useful strategy does not guarantee the skill to use it; possessing a skill does not guarantee its deployment under stress, fatigue, distraction, social pressure, or limited resources.

A useful initial diagnostic model is:

**Observed performance = f(knowledge, skills/capabilities, motivation, regulation, context, resources, health/state, task demands, feedback, and chance).**

This is a conceptual checklist, not a validated equation or a claim that the factors are independent. Avoid reducing every failure to weak discipline or character. Barriers may include missing knowledge, insufficient practice, illness, disability, exhaustion, unsafe environments, poverty, poor task design, lack of support, or conflicting demands.

## 6. Capability model: from definition to evidence

For each capability HDS investigates, record:

1. **Construct:** What exactly is being studied? Avoid broad labels without an operational definition.
2. **Boundary:** What is included and excluded? Which neighboring constructs may be confused with it?
3. **Mechanisms:** What cognitive, emotional, behavioral, physiological, interpersonal, or environmental processes may contribute?
4. **Context:** In what situations should the capability be expressed? Under what constraints?
5. **Observable indicators:** What behavior, task performance, report, or physiological measure could provide evidence?
6. **Measurement quality:** Reliability, validity, sensitivity to change, measurement error, and possible bias.
7. **Development:** What learning or adaptation processes might change the capability?
8. **Transfer:** Does improvement generalize beyond the trained task, setting, coach, or test?
9. **Durability:** Does it persist after the intervention ends?
10. **Trade-offs and harms:** What costs, adverse effects, or unintended consequences may arise?

A single test rarely captures a broad capability. Prefer multiple measures and real-world outcomes where feasible.

## 7. Research and evidence states

Every HDS output must carry an explicit epistemic status:

- **Idea:** a question or possibility, not yet adequately reviewed.
- **Hypothesis:** a falsifiable proposed explanation or prediction.
- **Research proposal:** a planned method for testing a question.
- **Evidence located:** relevant sources were found; quality and completeness remain to be assessed.
- **Preliminary evidence:** limited, indirect, inconsistent, or methodologically constrained evidence.
- **Evidence-supported within scope:** convergent evidence supports a bounded claim for a defined population, context, and outcome.
- **Replicated / independently corroborated:** independent work supports the result; specify what was replicated.
- **Unresolved / contradicted:** evidence is insufficient, conflicting, or weighs against the claim.

AI-generated synthesis, novelty suggestions, and hypotheses are research assistance — not discoveries, proof, or independent scientific review. A source's existence does not mean its claims are reliable. Preserve provenance and distinguish direct findings from interpretation.

## 8. Intervention development cycle

A proposed HDS intervention should follow this sequence:

1. Define a specific real-world problem and target population.
2. Specify the capability and operational definition.
3. Review existing constructs, interventions, measures, and evidence.
4. State a theory of change: what is expected to change, through which mechanism, and why.
5. Define outcomes, process measures, safety indicators, and possible harms in advance.
6. Design a feasible pilot with appropriate comparison where possible.
7. Measure adherence, acceptability, fidelity, and adverse events as well as outcomes.
8. Analyze uncertainty, missing data, subgroup differences, and alternative explanations.
9. Test transfer and durability rather than relying only on immediate task improvement.
10. Publish limitations and enable independent scrutiny and replication.

For research involving people, obtain appropriate ethics review, informed consent, privacy protections, and safeguarding before recruitment or data collection.

## 9. Guardrails

- Do not describe HDS as a validated science or established profession at this stage.
- Do not present AI-generated ideas as new discoveries.
- Do not infer causation from correlation, uncontrolled before–after change, or testimonials.
- Do not use a training score as a complete measure of a person's worth or character.
- Do not assume that discomfort, exhaustion, or danger automatically produces growth.
- Do not equate resilience with suppressing emotion, refusing help, or tolerating preventable harm.
- Do not generalize beyond the population, setting, intervention, and outcomes actually studied.
- Make uncertainty, conflicts of interest, limitations, and negative results visible.

## 10. Initial research priorities

The first research program should remain narrow enough to be executable. Candidate areas:

1. Self-regulation and goal-directed action in everyday contexts.
2. The gap between knowing a strategy, being capable of using it, and using it under pressure.
3. How practice, feedback, habits, and environmental design affect sustained behavior.
4. Transfer: whether trained capabilities generalize to untrained real-life situations.
5. Measurement: how to assess mental/behavioral capabilities without collapsing distinct constructs into one vague “mental toughness” score.

These are candidate priorities, not claims that the field has already established answers.

## 11. Minimum record for every HDS project

Every project should identify:
- Research question and rationale
- Target population and context
- Construct definitions
- Existing evidence and search date
- Hypotheses and competing explanations
- Study/intervention design
- Measures and outcome definitions
- Ethics and safety plan
- Analysis plan
- Results, uncertainty, limitations, and deviations
- Data/source provenance
- Current evidence state and next independent test

**Versioning rule:** Changes to this framework should be dated and justified. Preserve prior versions so conceptual drift can be examined rather than silently overwritten.
