# HDS Flagship Research Program 01 — From Knowing to Doing

**Program ID:** HDS-MENTAL-001  
**Status:** Candidate research program / protocol-development stage  
**Evidence status:** Not yet reviewed as a complete systematic or scoping review; no HDS empirical findings.  
**Primary domain:** Mental and behavioral capability  
**Framework links:** HDS Conceptual Framework v1.0; HDS Mental Capability Map v1.0

## 1. Research problem

People may know a useful strategy, explain why it matters, and demonstrate that they can perform it under favorable conditions, yet fail to use it consistently in everyday life. This gap is often described informally as a lack of discipline or willpower. HDS will not assume that explanation.

The program asks which person-level, task-level, and context-level factors help explain the difference between:
1. Knowing what to do.
2. Demonstrating the capability under defined conditions.
3. Using the behavior repeatedly in real life.

The program begins by mapping existing science. It must not assume this question is novel.

## 2. Primary research question

**Among people pursuing a self-selected, non-clinical everyday behavior goal, which factors are associated with the gap between strategy knowledge, demonstrated task capability, and repeated real-world enactment over time?**

This question is deliberately broad at the program level. A specific study must narrow the population, behavior, observation window, and measures before data collection.

## 3. Secondary questions

- Does strategy knowledge predict real-world enactment after accounting for demonstrated capability?
- Does demonstrated capability predict enactment when opportunity and context are considered?
- How are implementation planning, habit strength, self-efficacy, motivation, emotion regulation, and executive control related to enactment?
- How much within-person variation is associated with context, fatigue, competing demands, and environmental cues?
- Do the strongest predictors differ between starting a behavior, maintaining it, and resuming after interruption?
- Does improvement on a trained task transfer to an untrained everyday context?

## 4. Construct boundaries

The following are candidate explanatory constructs, not interchangeable labels:

| Construct | Initial working role | Boundary to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy knowledge | What a person knows or can explain | Does not prove skill or execution |
| Demonstrated capability | Performance on a specified task under specified conditions | May not generalize to daily life |
| Real-world enactment | Repeated observable behavior in a defined context | Influenced by opportunity and context |
| Executive control | Cognitive processes supporting goal-directed action | Not synonymous with willpower |
| Motivation / goal value | Reasons and value attached to action | Motivation is not capability |
| Self-efficacy | Belief about capability in a given setting | Belief can be miscalibrated |
| Implementation planning | Plans linking cues/situations to actions | A plan does not guarantee execution |
| Habit | Learned cue–response regularity | Not identical to deliberate self-control |
| Emotion regulation / coping | Processes for responding to emotional demands | Not equivalent to suppressing emotion |
| Context / opportunity | External conditions enabling or constraining action | Must not be treated as an individual trait |

Definitions must be refined against primary literature and authoritative reviews before measures are selected.

## 5. Competing explanatory models

The research should compare alternatives rather than assume one universal cause.

- **H1 — Knowledge-limitation model:** insufficient or inaccurate strategy knowledge explains a meaningful share of non-enactment.
- **H2 — Capability-limitation model:** people know the strategy but cannot reliably perform the required action under relevant conditions.
- **H3 — Regulation / motivation model:** people have knowledge and capability, but goal value, motivation, attention, emotion regulation, or competing impulses predict whether they act.
- **H4 — Context / opportunity model:** environmental cues, time, resources, social conditions, task friction, and competing demands explain substantial variation beyond person-level variables.
- **H5 — Dynamic interaction model:** enactment reflects interactions among knowledge, capability, motivation, regulation, context, and prior learning; no single factor is sufficient.

These are testable candidate models, not established facts. The protocol must specify measurable predictions that could disconfirm each model.

## 6. Proposed research sequence

### Phase 0 — Evidence map and scoping review
No participant recruitment.

Tasks:
1. Search databases using reproducible search strings and date ranges.
2. Identify existing theories and constructs that already address intention–behavior gaps, self-regulation, habits, implementation intentions, self-efficacy, executive function, coping, and behavior change.
3. Identify validated measures and their populations, language versions, psychometric properties, and limitations.
4. Separate longitudinal, experimental, observational, qualitative, and review evidence.
5. Map contradictions, measurement gaps, and contexts not adequately studied.
6. Ask an independent reviewer to challenge the search strategy and construct map.

Deliverables:
- Search protocol and search log
- Screening criteria and flow record
- Construct dictionary with provenance
- Evidence table and risk-of-bias plan appropriate to included designs
- Research gap memo distinguishing genuine gaps from gaps caused by narrow searches

### Phase 1 — Protocol specification
Before collecting data:
- Choose one narrowly defined everyday behavior and target population.
- Define the observation period and minimum number of observations.
- Specify how knowledge, capability, opportunity, and enactment will be measured separately.
- Select measures with evidence appropriate to the target population and language.
- Define primary and secondary outcomes.
- Predefine exclusions, missing-data handling, and analysis.
- Establish privacy, data minimization, consent, and safety procedures.
- Obtain ethics review where required before recruitment or data collection.
- Register or timestamp the protocol and analysis plan where appropriate.

### Phase 2 — Feasibility pilot
A pilot should assess whether the measures and repeated observations are understandable, acceptable, safe, and feasible. It should not be presented as proof of efficacy or as validation of a new HDS scale.

### Phase 3 — Observational study
If justified by Phase 0 and 1, prospectively measure knowledge, demonstrated capability, contextual opportunity, and repeated behavior. Analyze within-person and between-person variation separately where the design supports it.

### Phase 4 — Intervention study
Only after the observational model and evidence review justify a target:
- Select an intervention with an explicit mechanism.
- Compare it with an appropriate control or comparator.
- Measure process, outcome, adverse effects, and adherence.
- Test retention and transfer.
- Report uncertainty and null results.

## 7. Measurement principles

The central measurement requirement is **separation of levels**.

### Knowledge
Use a behavior-specific knowledge assessment with scoring criteria defined before use. Avoid treating confidence in an answer as correctness.

### Demonstrated capability
Use a standardized task that represents the relevant action. Record conditions, instructions, assistance, performance criteria, and measurement error.

### Real-world enactment
Where feasible, use repeated behavioral records rather than a single retrospective question. Minimize intrusion and collect only necessary data. Self-report may be useful but should be identified as self-report.

### Context and opportunity
Record whether the behavior was realistically possible, whether the relevant cue occurred, and what barriers or competing demands were present. A missed behavior when opportunity did not exist should not automatically count as a capability failure.

### Potential predictors
Use validated, population-appropriate instruments where available. Do not create an HDS-branded questionnaire merely because an existing scale is inconvenient. Any new instrument requires construct definition, item development, cognitive interviewing, psychometric evaluation, and independent validation.

## 8. Analysis principles

The analysis plan must be designed after the study design and measurement schedule are specified. Candidate approaches may include:
- Descriptive distributions and missingness patterns.
- Within-person versus between-person variation.
- Multilevel models for repeated observations, if justified by sample and design.
- Time-to-event or transition models for initiation and resumption, if appropriate.
- Sensitivity analyses for alternative definitions of opportunity and adherence.
- Measurement error and uncertainty analysis.

Do not interpret predictive association as causation. Do not choose models solely because they are sophisticated. Avoid overfitting, unplanned subgroup claims, and composite scores without a defensible measurement model.

## 9. Falsification and stop conditions

The program must revise or abandon its preferred explanation if:
- Existing research already answers the question with adequate quality and relevance.
- Proposed constructs cannot be distinguished with available measures.
- The behavior cannot be observed reliably or ethically.
- Opportunity and context cannot be measured well enough to interpret non-enactment.
- The proposed predictors fail to improve out-of-sample prediction over a simpler baseline.
- Findings do not replicate or do not transfer beyond the study setting.
- The intervention creates harms or unacceptable burdens.

## 10. What would count as progress?

Progress is not a dramatic story or a new label. It is:
- A reproducible map of existing evidence.
- Clearer construct boundaries.
- A defensible measurement strategy.
- A preregistered, ethically reviewed study where required.
- Transparent results with uncertainty.
- Independent scrutiny and replication.
- Evidence that an intervention changes a meaningful real-world outcome, with acceptable harms and demonstrated retention/transfer.

## 11. Immediate next tasks

1. Run a focused search for intention–behavior gaps, implementation intentions, habit formation, self-regulation, and ecological measurement of everyday behavior.
2. Identify major theories and reviews before designing a new HDS model.
3. Create a source-level evidence table with study design, population, behavior, measures, follow-up, findings, and limitations.
4. Draft a screening protocol and inclusion/exclusion criteria.
5. Obtain independent review of the proposed scope.
6. Only then select the first behavior and study design.

**Boundary:** This document is a research-program charter. It is not a human-subjects protocol ready for recruitment, a clinical intervention, or evidence that HDS has discovered a new construct.
