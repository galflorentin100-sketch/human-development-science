# HDS Evidence Scan 001 — Intention, Self-Regulation, and Real-World Action

**Program:** HDS-MENTAL-001 — From Knowing to Doing  
**Status:** Initial targeted evidence scan; not a systematic review and not a complete evidence synthesis.  
**Search date:** 2026-11-04  
**Scope:** Intention–behavior gap, implementation intentions, naturalistic self-control, ecological measurement, habit formation, and behavior change.

## 1. Purpose and limitations

This scan identifies relevant reviews and meta-analyses to orient the HDS flagship research program. It is not exhaustive: search strings, databases, screening decisions, full-text eligibility, duplicate handling, and risk-of-bias appraisal have not yet been completed. Abstract-level descriptions are not enough to grade certainty or make broad causal claims.

The sources below should be treated as evidence leads. Before formal conclusions, retrieve and inspect full texts, methods, supplements, and relevant primary studies. Confirm publication metadata and eligibility independently.

## 2. Initial evidence leads

### E001 — Intention–behavior gap in physical activity
**Source:** Rhodes, R. E. et al. (2023), *The intention-behaviour gap in physical activity: a systematic review and meta-analysis of the action control framework*. British Journal of Sports Medicine.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2022-106640  
**PubMed:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37460164/

**Design / scope:** Systematic review and meta-analysis; 22 articles, 25 independent samples, total N=29,600; physical activity intention and behavior.
**Reported result:** 33.0% were unsuccessful intenders and 38.7% successful intenders. The estimated intention–behavior gap among intenders was 47.6%.
**Interpretation:** In this physical-activity literature, positive intention did not reliably translate into behavior. This supports studying enactment separately from intention.
**Limits:** Specific to physical activity and the review's operational definitions and included samples. It does not establish the same gap size in other behaviors, nor explain each individual's non-enactment.
**HDS use:** Strong justification for measuring intention and actual behavior separately in a narrowly specified behavior domain.

### E002 — Implementation intentions: updated meta-analysis
**Source:** 2024/2025 publication, *The when and how of planning: Meta-analysis of the scope and components of implementation intentions in 642 tests*. European Review of Social Psychology.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563  
**Publisher page:** https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563

**Design / scope:** Meta-analysis across 642 independent tests, covering cognitive, affective, and behavioral outcomes.
**Reported result:** The abstract reports effects in the range d=.27 to d=.66; larger effects were reported when plans used contingent if–then formats, participants were highly motivated, and plans were rehearsed.
**Interpretation:** Planning can be a useful self-regulation strategy; the form and preparation of plans may matter.
**Limits:** A broad set of outcomes and tests is not the same as one uniform intervention or a guarantee of real-world behavior change. Full-text methods, heterogeneity, publication bias, and outcome groupings require review.
**HDS use:** Include implementation planning as a candidate mechanism/intervention, not as a universal solution.

### E003 — Naturalistic assessment of self-control
**Source:** *A scoping review of naturalistic assessments of self-control* (2025), Personality and Individual Differences, 241, 113185.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2025.113185  
**Publisher page:** https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0191886925001473  
**Open full-text record:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12165334/

**Design / scope:** Preregistered scoping review of repeated assessment of self-control in everyday life.
**Reported result:** The review notes that self-control is historically measured as a trait, while fewer studies examine momentary within-person variation in naturalistic contexts. It reports meaningful variance at the within-person level.
**Interpretation:** A person's average trait score may not describe how their self-control changes across situations and moments.
**Limits:** The review describes a relatively nascent body of work; the number and comparability of naturalistic studies remain important constraints.
**HDS use:** Consider repeated, context-linked measurements instead of relying only on one-time trait questionnaires.

### E004 — Naturalistic assessment of inhibitory control
**Source:** Dinu, L. et al. (2024), *Naturalistic assessments across the lifespan: Systematic review of inhibition measures in ecological settings*. Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews, 167, 105915.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neubiorev.2024.105915  
**Open full text:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11870848/

**Design / scope:** Systematic review; 64 studies using gamification, virtual reality, or brief repeated ecological assessments.
**Reported result:** Naturalistic tasks were generally feasible and acceptable and often comparable with standardized equivalents, but tasks and psychometric reporting were heterogeneous.
**Interpretation:** Ecological assessment is feasible in some settings, but measurement quality and construct specificity need careful attention.
**Limits:** Different methods may measure different mixtures of inhibition and other cognitive domains; comparability is limited.
**HDS use:** Develop context-sensitive measures only after construct definition and psychometric review.

### E005 — Self-regulation mechanisms in health behavior change
**Source:** *Self-regulation mechanisms in health behavior change: a systematic meta-review of meta-analyses, 2006–2017* (PubMed record).  
**PubMed:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31662031/

**Design / scope:** Meta-review of 66 meta-analyses of interventions and health behavior/outcomes.
**Reported result:** Only a small proportion of included meta-analyses directly examined whether changes in self-regulation predicted behavior change; many assessed intervention components instead.
**Interpretation:** Evidence that an intervention component is effective does not automatically establish the mechanism through which behavior changed.
**Limits:** The review covers meta-analyses published 2006–2017 and health behavior; it does not resolve all domains of self-regulation.
**HDS use:** In future intervention studies, measure proposed mechanisms and outcomes separately; do not infer mechanism from outcome change alone.

### E006 — Temporal self-regulation theory
**Source:** Dorina et al. (2023), *Utility of temporal self-regulation theory in health and social behaviours: A meta-analysis*. British Journal of Health Psychology, 28(2), 397–438.  
**DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/bjhp.12631  
**Publisher page:** https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/bjhp.12631

**Design / scope:** Meta-analysis of 37 eligible articles with 12,555 participants assessing at least two of the theory's constructs.
**Reported purpose:** Evaluate predictive utility of temporal self-regulation theory for behavior and the intention–behavior gap.
**Interpretation:** Existing theoretical models already attempt to explain intention–behavior translation; HDS must compare with these rather than assume a conceptual vacuum.
**Limits:** Full-text results and construct operationalizations need extraction before stating the strength of each association.
**HDS use:** Include temporal self-regulation theory among candidate frameworks for the full evidence map.

### E007 — Habit formation in health behavior
**Source:** *Time to Form a Habit: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Health Behaviour Habit Formation and Its Determinants*.  
**Open full text:** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/

**Design / scope:** Systematic review and meta-analysis of health-behavior habit formation, with diverse behaviors, interventions, and study designs.
**Reported scope:** Included behaviors such as physical activity, nutrition, water consumption, flossing, and sedentary behavior; studies used varied follow-up durations and habit measures.
**Interpretation:** Habit formation is relevant but cannot be reduced to one universal number of days; behavior, context, measurement, and study design vary.
**Limits:** Extract the exact pooled estimates, heterogeneity, and risk-of-bias assessment from the full article before citing quantitative conclusions.
**HDS use:** Treat habit automaticity and repeated behavior as distinct measures; avoid promising a fixed habit-formation timeline.

## 3. Preliminary synthesis — what this scan suggests

1. **Intention is not behavior.** At least in physical activity, a substantial intention–behavior gap is documented.
2. **Planning is a plausible intervention component.** If–then plans have a broad evidence base, but effects depend on conditions and outcomes.
3. **Self-control varies by situation.** Trait-level measures alone may miss meaningful within-person variation.
4. **Ecological measurement is promising but methodologically uneven.** Naturalistic tasks and repeated assessment can improve contextual relevance, but psychometric quality and construct boundaries remain essential.
5. **Mechanism claims are often weaker than outcome claims.** A behavior-change result does not by itself prove which psychological process caused it.
6. **HDS should not reinvent established constructs.** Existing theories of self-regulation, habit, planning, and action control are direct competitors and collaborators for the HDS model.

These are directional conclusions from a targeted scan, not a formal certainty assessment.

## 4. Research gaps worth testing — candidate, not proven novelty

- How do knowledge, demonstrated capability, and real-world enactment jointly predict repeated behavior in the same people?
- What is the incremental contribution of context and opportunity beyond trait-level self-control and motivation?
- Which measurements can distinguish inability, lack of opportunity, deliberate non-prioritization, and momentary regulation difficulty?
- Does improvement in a standardized self-control task transfer to a meaningful everyday behavior?
- When do if–then plans help, and for whom do they fail or become counterproductive?
- How do initiation, maintenance, lapse, and resumption differ as behavioral processes?

A gap in this short scan is not evidence that no one has studied it. Each candidate requires forward/backward citation searching and broader database coverage.

## 5. Methodological recommendations for HDS

- Choose one observable behavior before selecting a scale.
- Measure intention, knowledge, demonstrated task capability, opportunity, and actual behavior separately.
- Use repeated measures when the question concerns variation across time or contexts.
- Prefer validated measures and inspect population/language applicability.
- Combine self-report with objective or independently recorded behavior where feasible, while acknowledging that objective measures also have error.
- Predefine outcomes, time windows, and analysis.
- Separate within-person effects from between-person differences.
- Record missingness, adherence, context, burden, and adverse effects.
- Avoid constructing a global "mental strength" score from heterogeneous measures.
- Obtain ethics review and consent before collecting data from people where required.

## 7. Decision

**Do not yet create an HDS-specific self-control or discipline scale.** First map existing constructs and measures, and determine whether the research question can be answered using established instruments and repeated behavioral observation. Develop a new measure only if a defensible construct or measurement gap remains after independent review.
